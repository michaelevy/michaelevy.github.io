Ngā tūwāhi (Locative nouns)

1/ Ngā tūwāhi (Locative nouns). Rāwāhi/tāwāhi - the other side, from the speaker's perspective. I titiro ia ki tāwāhi o te awa. She looked across to the other side of the river. Kei rāwāhi o te pātiki rāua e tū ana. They're standing on the other side of the field.

1/ Ngā tūwāhi (Locative nouns). Roto - in, inside. Kei roto i te whare. In(side) the house. In some dialects roto can be shortened to rō which is then followed immediately by another noun. Kei rō whare ia. She's inside. Haere mai ki rō whare. Come inside (the house). Usually Eastern Māori - Bay of Plenty - East Cape - down to Kahungunu. But is now used across the country more and more

1/ Ngā tūwāhi (Locative nouns). Runga - on, on top of. Kei runga i te tēpu ō tiere. Kua pau katoa ērā atu i a au te kai. Your cherries are on the table. I ate all the rest. Followed by ake it means 'above', 'upwards'. Whakairia ki runga ake i te kūaha. Hang it above the door.

1/ Ngā tūwāhi (Locative nouns). Tai - sea, coast, tide . In modern Māori it is used as a noun. Kua pari te tai - It's high tide. Te Tai Rāwhiti - The East Coast. Tai mate - slack water (when the tide is turning). Wai tai - salt water, seawater. Tai nui - spring tide, king tide.

1/ Ngā tūwāhi (Locative nouns). Tua - beyond, the other side of (usually something solid). Kei tua o tērā puke te wāhi e kimi nā koe. The place you're looking for is over that hill.

1/ Ngā tūwāhi (Locative nouns). Hea/whea - where? Strictly speaking hea/whea should be used when the expected answer is a locative or placename. E haere ana koe ki whea? Where are you going to? Ki Te Whanganui-a-Tara mō tētahi anō o ngā hīkoi mautohe ki tēnei Kāwanatanga.

Ngā tūmahi (verbs)

1/ Ngā tūmahi (verbs). Tūmahi whiti - transitive verb. There are 3 types of tūmahi whiti in Māori. 1. Tūmahi hāoho - active verb. Here the object (who or what is receiving the action) is marked with either i or ki.

1/ Ngā tūmahi (verbs). Tūmahi whiti - transitive verb. There are 3 types of tūmahi whiti in Māori. 3. Pane akuaku (actor emphatic). The agent stand at the start of the sentence preceded by nā plus i before the verb (for the past), or mā plus e before the verb for the future.

1/ Ngā tūmahi (verbs). Tūmahi poro - intransitive verb. Tūmahi poro usually involve only the person or thing performing the action and the action itself. The action does not involve or affect anyone else. For example: Kei te haere ia - She is going. Kua noho ia. He sat down.

1/ Ngā tūmahi (verbs). Tūmahi oti - statives (or neuter verbs). A small group of verbs that refer to a state (as a result of an action), rather than the action itself. I pakaru tana waea. Her phone is busted. Kua oti te mahi. The job is completed.

Ngā tūāhua (adjectives)

1/ Ngā tūāhua (adjectives). Tūāhua are frequently found following a tumu (base word), be it noun or verb, which they qualify. He tangata pai. A good person. Hoki pai atu. Get home safely.

1/ Ngā tūāhua (adjectives). Tūāhua can also be use as nouns. Ohomauri ana au i te koi o ōna whakaaro. I was taken aback by the incisiveness of her thinking. Whakaorangia mātou i te kino o ērā kaupapahere, ture hoki. Deliver from the evil of those policies and laws. 2/ Comparative adjectives are made by adding the particles ake, atu or iho after the tūāhua, preceded sometimes by kē for extra emphasis. Kua reka (kē) atu tēnā i tāku i tumānako ai. That was sweeter than I had expected. 3/ In comparative statements 'i' is used for 'than'. Kua roa ake i te hāwhe haora au e tatari ana i konei, ā, kāore anō ia kia puta mai. I've been waiting here for longer than half an hour, and he still hasn't appeared.

1/ Ngā tūāhua (adjectives). Rawa can be added after an adjective to give the sense of 'too, overly, unduly'. He roa rawa tēnā tūwiri. Rapua he mea poto ake. That screw's too long. Look for a shorter one. Kua taikaha rawa tāu i kī ai. That was unduly harsh what you said. 2/ With the addition of 'atu' after rawa or following 'tino' it becomes the superlative, i.e. the most... Ko Aoraki te maunga teitei rawa atu o Aotearoa. Aoraki is New Zealand's highest mountain. Ko ia te tangata tino arero rua rawa kua tūtakina rawatia e au. He the most... 3/ hypocritical person I have ever met. Ko Waikato te awa roa rawa atu o Aotearoa. Waikato is the longest river in New Zealand. Note that the preposition 'o' is used in Māori before the object, person or group within which the comparison is being made, in this case Aotearoa.

Ngā tūkapi whakaata

1/ Ngā tūkapi whakaata - reflexive pronouns - These refer back to someone already mentioned. Look at this sentence - E kōrero ana ia ki a ia - This could mean s/he is talking to him/her, or it could mean S/he is talking to himself/herself. 2/ We don't really know (with no context). It is ambiguous. However with the addition of anō or ake, the meaning is clear. Kei te kōrero ia ki a ia anō - S/he is talking to herself/himself. Some more examples. Māna anō tērā e mahi - she will do that herself. 3/ Kua ako kē ki te tāwharu i a ia ake. She's already learnt to dress herself. Me mahi e koe anō, hei aha te tatari kia mahia e tētahi atu. You should do it yourself and not bother waiting for someone else (to do it).

Ngā tūkapi

1/ Ngā tūkapi - (personal) pronouns. Māori has 3 tūkapi takitahi (singular pronouns). Tūkapi kiritūnei (1st person pronoun) - au or ahau (I, me). The ahau version tends to be used more often in Northern Māori. Around Gisborne and Ngāti Porou you may hear 'awau' 2/ instead or 'au' or 'ahau'. Tūkapi kiritūnā (2nd person pronoun) - koe (you singular). Tūkapi kiritūrā (3rd person pronoun) - ia (he/she or it). Then there are the tūkapi takirua (dual pronouns) that refer to two people. There are 2 tūkapi takirua kiritūnei (first person dual pronouns) tāua and māua. Tāua (we two, including you) and māua (we two, excluding you, he/she and I). One helpful way of remembering - tāua starts with a 't' and 'takes in' the person you are talking to. Māua starts with am 'm' and 'misses' out the person you are talking to. The 'ua' on the end originally was rua - two. The dual pronouns all end in -ua. Logical, ne rā? More tomorrow.

1/ Ngā tūkapi - (personal) pronouns. Tūkapi takirua - dual pronouns continued. Kōrua - you (two). (You can think of this as as shortened form of koe+rua). Note than northen dialects often use kourua instead. Tēnā kōrua/tēnā kourua. 2/ and rāua - they, them (two). (Think of this as a combination of ia+rua which over the centuries has morphed into rāua.) Now for the tūkapi takitini - plural pronouns. Tātou/Tātau - all us (including the speaker). Mātou/mātau - they/them and me (but not you - excluding the... 3/ person being spoken to). Eastern Māori including the Bay of Plenty and East coast dialects and the South Island prefer the tātau and mātau forms. The -tou or -tau at the end was originally toru (three) which again has morphed over time. Orginally they were perhaps... 4/ trial pronous (referring to combinations of three people only). Trial pronouns are still used in a number of Pacific languages, including Fijian (i.e. 'kodou' is the Fijian for 'you three' - you can see the similarity to the Māori koutou. 5/ However in modern Māori the plural forms tātou/tātau and mātou/mātau refer to groups of three or more. Kia ora tātou katoa. Greetings to us all. E wehe ana mātau. Me haere tahi mai. We're leaving. You'd better come with us.

1/ Ngā tūkapi - (personal) pronouns. Tūkapi takitini - plural pronouns cont'd. That leaves 'koutou' - you (three or more) and rātou or rātau for they, them (3 or more). Eastern Māori including the Bay of Plenty and East coast dialects and the South Island prefer the rātau form. 2/ Note that Eastern Māori dialects in Te Ika-a-Māui often pronounce koutou as kōtou (even though they still write it as koutou). 3/ Northern dialects often use tāo, māo, rāo for tāua, māua, rāua particularly in animated and fast speech. 4/ Tribes in Te Moana-a-Toi area, in particular Te Arawa, often pronounce some plural pronouns with short vowels e.g, tatau, matau, ratau, even though using a macron when writing them.

1/ Ngā tūkapi - Coordination with pronouns - 'and'. In Māori you need to choose the pronoun that fits the whole group you're talking about, spelling out the members with a succession of phrases marked with 'ko'. 2/ Some examples: māua ko Hemi, i.e. there are two of us including Hemi. Mātou ko Hemi ko Mere ko Pita. Hemi, Mary, Peter and I/me. (Note: there are no commas before each ko phrase. However, English puctuation has intruded into Māori and many would add commas before ko.) 3/ Kōrua ko tō hoa - you and your mate. Koutou mā - you and the others. Rātou ko tōna ipo ko ōna mātua - He, his sweetheart, and her parents. (Rātou used because there are 3 or more people involved.) 4/ You can choose to use 'me' for 'and' instead of a prounoun. Kua tae mai ia me ana hoa - He and his friends arrived. Me, however, changes the meaning in Māori and has the sense of 'with' or 'in the company of'.

1/ Ngā tūkapi - using pronouns for things - In English 'it' and 'they' can be used for things. Māori pronouns are mostly used for people only. To refer to things, there are 2 strategies. 1. Leave out the reference (leave a gap). 2/ E.g. Tahi: Kei hea rā aku pūrari kī? Rua: E kī rā, kei runga i te tēpu, i te wāhi i waiho rā koe. One: Where the hell are my bloody keys? Two: OMG, on the table, where you left them. 2. Repeat the noun, preceded by 'taua'. 3/ Tahi: Kua mātakitaki koe i te punipuni tuarua o Squid Games i Netflix? Rua: E kāo, he whakarekereke rawa taua hōtaka mōku. One: Have you watched the second series of Squid Games on Netflix? Two: No, it's (that programme is) too violent for me. (More on this topic tomorrow.)

1/ Ngā tūkapi - using pronouns for things cont'd. - In some cases you can use pronouns for things or animals if they are treated as if they were people, that is personified. Ki te kitea ngā mea ora hou e te kaipūtaiao, ko te tikanga māna e hoatu ingoa ki a rātou e ai ki ngā... 2/ tikanga pūtaiao. If a scientist discovers new living things they customarily name them according to scientific conventions. Here rātou is not confined to people. Kua whakahoutia te whare nui rāua ko te whare kai o tērā marae. The meeting house and dining hall of that marae... 3/ have been refurbished. Here the meeting house and the dining hall are regarded as people (and may carry the names of ancestors), hence 'rāua ko' for 'and'. 4/ However, in modern Māori you will see pronouns being used more and more for things other than people – most likely due to the influence of English. Is this ok? However, languages are not static – they are always changing.

Unusual use of words of one class as another

1/ Unusual use of words of one class as another. Examples. Takurua - winter. But it is possible to say in Māori - I takurua ia i Whītī. He spent the winter/He wintered in Fiji. Koroua - elderly man. Kua koroua rawa au ki te koropiko rawa atu ki reira. 2/ I've grown too old to stoop right down there. (Speaking from experience). Mā hea means by what route. Here it's used as a verb. I mā hea mai koe? How did you get here (by what route)? Usually though, the initial 'i' is left off - Mā hea mai koe? 3/ And another most unusual example. Matapihitia atu tēnā pouaka - Pass that box out the window. Here matapihi (window) is used as a verb meaning to pass out (or in) through the window. You'll find lots more examples of these in classical Māori, marae language and karakia.

Personification

1/ Personification. Idiomatically many nouns can be personified and treated as if they were people. E hoa, kua rongo māua ko taringa - (literally, ear and I have heard) = Look, I have heard. You have sat down to a hākari (feast) and eaten your fill you could say to 2/ your hosts, by way of compliment - Kua puta a Pito - Navel has popped out. Note the personal 'a' and the capital letter. Kua riro ia i a Aituā - he has been taken by disaster and death - he was killed/he died in an accident. 3/ Conversely personal nouns can be used as common nouns. Kāore au i te mōhio ki tērā Hēmi e kōrero nā koe. I don't know that Hēmi you're talking about. Ehara tērā i te Mere e mōhio ana au. That's not the Mary I know.

One of the most versatile words in Māori is 'mea'

1/ One of the most versatile words in Māori is 'mea' - thing, reason, purpose; so-and-so; to say, put, do, try. Ka mea atu ia ki a Mea kia meatia ngā mea katoa ki mea wāhi. Engari nā te kōroiroi o mea kia pērā, ka mea anō ia, 'Meinga ināia tonu nei!' 2/ She told so-and-so to put everything in such-and-such place. But because of so-and-so's reluctance to do that, she said again, "Do it right now!' (Meinga is an alternative passive form, instead of meatia, for mea, the verb - do, put, say.) Enough of mea for the day!

Te tumu

1/ Te tumu - nucleus of a sentence. In older texts there are examples of a phrase being used as a tumu of a sentence. Examples. I rā moana mai rātou - They came by way of the sea. I mā runga pahi rātou ki Paeroa. They went to Paeroa by bus. 2/ In modern Māori these would most likely be: I haere rātou mā runga poti. I haere rātou ki Paeroa mā runga pahi (or something similar).

Tūingoa + tūāhua (noun + adjective)

1/ Tūingoa + tūāhua (noun + adjective). Traditionally Māori only allowed one adjective to qualify a noun, e.g, he whare nui - a big house. If you wanted to add a second adjective, there are a number of ways. For a 'big red house' you can say 'he whare nui, whero (anō) hoki' 2/ Or - He whare nui, he whare whero – repeating the phrase and substituting the adjective, or use a second phrase with 'mea' - He whare nui, he mea whero. Nowadays however it will most likely be: He whare nui me te whero. English often qualifies an noun with multiple adjectives. 3/ And, probably due to the pervasive nature of English, Māori now can have multiple adjectives qualifying a noun, separated by commas, and often with 'me te' before the last one. He tangata tāroaroa, tūpuhi, whakamanea me te hūmārie. 4/ A tall, thin, charismatic, (and) humble person.

Tūmahi + tūāhua (verb + adjective)

1/ Tūmahi + tūāhua (verb + adjective). In Māori, you can qualify a verb with an adjective such as kōrero tere - speak fast, and hoki pai atu - return safe, as in American English. (In NZ English, however, we would use an adverb instead - 'speak quickly' and 'return safely'.) 2/ Māori does not often do that, however. Rather than saying: E mahi pai ana ia - he works well, Māori would use a noun phrase and say He pai tana mahi (His work is good). Other examples. He tere rawa tana kōrero mai - He spoke too fast (to me). He tere ia ki te kōrero - She ... 3/ speaks fast (i.e. She is fast at talking). One way it is acceptable to qualify a verb with an adjective is when it's in the passive form, and in that case the adjective takes a passive suffix. I rangona paitia ia (e au) ki aku oko whakamōroto hou. I heard him well with my new 4/ hearing aids. You can even leave out the passive suffix on the verb when there is one on the adjective, and say correctly - I rongo paitia ia (e au) ki aku oko whakamōroto hou.

Tūmahi whiti + tūingoa (transitive verb + noun)

1/ Tūmahi whiti + tūingoa (transitive verb + noun). In Māori, you can add a noun to transitive verb. E.g. tito waiata - compose songs, kai kōura - eat crayfish. In this case, the speaker has no definite song or crayfish in mind, just a general statement. 2/ Me hoko rawa rāua i tō rāua whare nā te korenga e taea te utu te mōkete. Kei te kimi whare anō rāua, he mea ngāwari ake te utu. They had to sell their house 'cause they couldn't meet the mortgage. They're house hunting again, for a cheaper one. 'Kimi whare' here, as they are 3/ not looking for a particular one, just a cheaper one. This process is called object incorporation - the object is added to the verb. However if you are referring to specific objects, you use an object phrase marked by 'i'. E.g. I tito ia i ngā waiata e rua inapō. He composed 4/ the two songs last night. He momo tonu, i kai ia i te kōura nui rawa. True to form, he ate the biggest crayfish.

Phrases with katoa

1/ Phrases with katoa. As a noun it can be used at the start of a phrase. Katoa ngā tāngata i tae atu i manaaki paitia. Everybody who arrived was well looked after. It can also be used with 'te'. Kua kotahi te katoa o Kānata ki te ātete i ā Trump tākehoko tāwāhi kaupēhi. 2/ All of Canada are united in their opposition to Trump's punishing trade tariffs. Mostly though, it follows the noun, rather like an adjective. Kāore anō ngā pirimia katoa o Aotearoa kia kounu i te haere ki Waitangi ina kōkirikiritia e ngāi Māori. Not all prime ministers... 3/ chicken out of going to Waitangi when under pressure from Māori. It can follow a verb. Kua tae katoa mai rātou, hāunga ngā moeroa. They've all arrived, apart from the sleepyheads. Or like this. Kei konei katoa (nei) tātou, ki te hoe! We're all here, let's get on with it!

Phrases with haere

1/ Phrases with haere. Haere can be added after adjectives or statives (neuter verbs, e.g. pau) to denote a progressive increase in a state or attribute. Kua pau katoa aku moni, kua pakaru te pūkoro. My money's all gone, I'm broke. With haere - Kua pau haere taku moni,... 2/ ā, kaore anō kia utua te nama hiko mō tērā marama. My money's running out, and I haven't paid last month's power bill yet. Kua ora haere mai anō ia i muri i te hauata. She's getting better again after the accident (the implication here is that she could improve further yet). 3/ After other verbs it can imply that the activity is carried out as one goes around. I hariru haere ia i te iwi kāinga. He went around shaking hands with the locals. E tangi haere mai ana te ope. The group are weeping as they come. 4/ Sometimes it seems redundant as in - Kei te piki haere ngā utu. Prices are increasing. Following a passive verb, it agrees. Kua tīhore(tia) haeretia ngā papa gib i muri i te waipuke. The gib panels have been progressively stripped out after the flood.

More on 'hanga'

1/ More on 'hanga'. Idioms - te hanga a te - there were lots of, many. I reira te hanga a te taiohi e whakarārangi ana me te tūmanako kia hopukina e te kiriawe Tik Tok. There were lots of youngsters there posing hoping to be videoed by the Tik Tok influencer. 2/ Ehara i te hanga - it's not something insignificant, it's no ordinary thing, it's pretty amazing. Ehara i te hanga tō Trump hiahia ki te taunaha i Kānata. It's no ordinary thing Trump's desire to annex Canada. Ko te hanga e.. just so long as. Ko te hanga e kore au e waiho... 3/ ki muri - Just so long as I'm not left behind. Hanga aroha (ana) - it's heartbreaking, it's sad, it's tragic, it's heart-rending, it's pitiful. Can be real as in - Hanga aroha ana kua turakina tana whare e te huripari. It's tragic that the hurricane destroyed her home. 4. Or it can be sarcastic. Hanga aroha i tangohia tana hopuoro. It's tragic his microphone was taken away.

Modifiers preceding verbs and adjectives

1/ Modifiers preceding verbs and adjectives. Āhua -quite, fairly, somewhat. I whakaaro ia he āhua pai ngā whakaritenga, taea noatia te tangohanga atu o tana hopuoro. He thought the arrangements were quite good until his microphone was taken away. 2/ Before numbers, āhua means 'about, approximately'. Āhua whā tekau ngā tāngata i tae atu ki te hui. About forty people arrived at the meeting. 3/ In some areas, i.e. Te Taitokerau, hanga can be used as an alternative to 'āhua'. I hanga pai te hui, mā te aha anō kāore tētahi tangata i reira e ngongoro ana. The meeting was ok, at least there was nobody there snoring. More on 'hanga' tomorrow.

1/ Modifiers preceding verbs and adjectives. Āta - carefully, deliberately, intently. Me āta haere. Go carefully. I āta kai ia i aku rare. He deliberately ate my lollies. I āta whakamātau ia ki te taraiwa i tētahi waka takerau ki runga i ngā kaupae ki te whare pāremata. 2/ He deliberately tried to drive a jeep up the steps to parliament. Sometimes, it can mean 'slowly'. Nā Māui i here te rā kia āta haere ai. Māui tied the sun so it would go slowly. At other times it can mean 'clearly'. 3/ Kīhai rāua i āta mōhio ki te ara haerenga ki te awa. They clearly didn't know the route to the river. Nā te kōrehu kāore au i āta kite te motu. Because of the haze I didn't see the island clearly.

1/ Modifiers preceding verbs and adjectives. Kaha - to such an extent. I tino whakamā rātou ki tōna kaha pōrangi, me te whakamā anō ki a rātou anō mō te pōti i a ia. They were truly ashamed at his downright stupidity, and at themselves for voting him in. 2/ Nā tana kaha whai i tāna i hiahia ai, ka ea. Her resolute pursuit of what she wanted, paid off. I te kaha mamae o tana waewae, ka waea atu ki te rata me te tūmanako kia kaua e tatari mō te toru wiki mō te whakaritenga kite i te rata. Because his leg was so sore, he rang the.. 3/ doctor in the hope he wouldn't have to wait three weeks for an appointment.

1/ Modifiers preceding verbs and adjectives. Mātua - implies that something should/was/will be carried out first. I mātua waea ia ki a ia, i mua i te pekanga atu. He telephoned her first, before calling in. 2/ Usually though it is used in commands. Me mātua kōrero ki a ia i mua i te whāki ki tōna hoa wahine. You must speak to him first, before informing his girlfriend. 3/ Sometimes it is found in constructions like 'in order to achieve... you must first...' E taea ai tēnei te whakatau, me mātua matapaki ngā take ki te hapori. In order to resolve this, you must first discuss the issues with the community.

Modifiers for verbs and adjectives

1/ Modifiers for verbs and adjectives. Paku - a little. Haere tonu, ki kō paku atu, ki kō paku atu, anā, kua tae, hurō! Keep going, a little bit futher, a little bit futher, there, you've arrived, hurray! 2/ When added to the front of verb it has the effect of downplaying, or being modest. Ki te paku āwhina mai koe, ko te painga atu tērā. If you just give me a little bit of help, I'd really appreciate it. He paku raruraru tōku. I have a little bit of a problem. (A big problem... 3/ really, but I'm downplaying it.) In negative sentences used before a verb = in the slightest, at all. Kīhai ia i paku aro mai ki tāki i kī ai, me te aha i tino puku taku rae. He didn't take the slightest notice of what I said, so I was really pissed off. Aroha mai. It should be: Kīhai ia i paku aro mai ki tāku i kī ai,....

1/ Modifiers for verbs and adjectives. Pīki - big. A transliteration of the English. Used informally in expressions, sometimes humourously. He pīki raruraru tō tātou. We have a big problem/We're in the pooh alright. Pīkiwhara (big fella) - He pīkiwhara rawa tēnā kōnae. That... 2/ file is far too large. Māori quite often uses transliterations humorously. Kua peke atu ia ki te kerēwa. He's jumped up to clever. (Implies pretentiousness, pretending to be more clever than he is). 3/ Sometimes English and Māori are blended into new words (houmorously). Kua makachilly i waho. It's cold outside. (A blend of makariri and chilly.)

1/ Modifiers for verbs and adjectives. Tata - nearly, almost ,when used before verbs. Kua tata oti tana kauhau te tuhi. He's almost finished writing his lecture. Kua tata pau taku moni, engari ehara tērā i te rerekē. My money's almost gone, but there nothing unusual in that.

1/ Modifiers for verbs and adjectives. Tē - not. An old negative form often heard in fomulaic expressions. Te moenga tē whakaarahia - The sleep from which there is no awakening. When there is a particle immediately following the possessive determiner. Tōku nei whare - this house of mine. Tāu nā pukapuka - that book of yours. Āna ake tamariki - her own children. Ōu anō pūkenga - your own skills. 3/ Note- If there's no particle immediately after the posessive the neutral forms can be used. Tō pukapuka - your book. Taku whare - my house. Ana tamariki ake - her own children. Ō pūkenga anō - your own skills.

Personal article

1/ Personal article - A. 1. Used before personal nouns when there is no preposition. A Rewi - Dave. A wai - who. 2. Before personal nouns preceded by i, ki, kei, and hei. Ki a Hūhana - to Susan. Determiner

1/ Determiners – He (2). With the exception of 'me' (me he manu rere ahau - if I were a bird), 'he' cannot be used after a preposition. For the sentence - He's looking for a new employee, there are three ways of conveying this in Māori. 1. Kei te kimi kaimahi hou ia. 2. Kei te... 2/ kimihia he kaimahi hou (e ia). or 3. Kei te kimi ia i tētahi kaimahi hou. This last is the most common method in modern Māori. After the 'i', which is a preposition in Maori that marks the object, 'he' cannot be used. 3. However, some speakers may say - Kei te kimi ia he kaimahi hou - dropping the 'i'. This is no doubt a construction influenced by English, an anglicism. To many Māori speakers it is ungrammatical.

1/ Determiner. Ia - each, every. Maranga ai rāua ia rā, ia rā i te ono karaka. They get up every day at 6 o'clock. It is usual to repeat the ia phrase, but not necessary. Some speakers may preface ia rā with 'i' - i ia rā, i ia rā. In modern Māori people sometimes say... 2/ (i) ia te rā, (i) ia te rā - inserting a 'te'. Order of Particles

1/ Order of Particles. They usually go after the word they refer to in this order: 1. Manner particles (kau, kē, mā, noa, rawa, tonu). 2. Directional particles (mai, atu, ake, iho). 3. Locative particles (nei, nā, rā, ai, ana). 4. Others (anō, hoki, anake, koa, rānei, pea). 2/ Mai and rā anō can occur as 'mai rā anō' or 'rā anō mai' but with a slight change of meaning. Nō tana hokinga rā anō mai ki te kāinga, ka mōhio pū ki te mahi tika māna. It wasn't until she returned home that she knew what would be the right job for her. Mai rā anō i tana... 3/ ohinga, kua mōhio kē he aha tana mahi ina pakeke. Right from when she was a child she knew what her job would be as an adult. (ohinga = childhood)
